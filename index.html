1. Sampling: Capturing a Moment in Time

What is Sampling?

In digital signal processing, sampling is the process of taking discrete values (samples) from a continuous signal at specific intervals. It's the first step in converting an analog signal, like a sound wave, into a digital format that a computer can understand.

Sampling Frequency

The sampling frequency (or sample rate) is how often we take a sample. It's measured in Hertz (Hz). For example, CD-quality audio is sampled at 44,100 Hz, meaning 44,100 samples are taken every second.

The Nyquist-Shannon Theorem

This fundamental theorem states that to accurately reconstruct a signal, the sampling frequency (fs) must be at least twice the highest frequency component of the signal (fmax).

fs ≥ 2 * fmax
If you sample too slowly (below the Nyquist rate), you get an effect called aliasing, where the reconstructed signal is a distorted, lower-frequency version of the original.

2. Quantization: Assigning a Value

What is Quantization?

After sampling, we have a series of measurements. Quantization is the process of "rounding" each of these measurements to the nearest value within a finite set of discrete levels. Think of it as mapping an infinite number of possible sample values onto a limited number of steps.

Bit Depth and Quantization Levels

The number of available levels is determined by the bit depth. An n-bit system can represent 2n levels.

  • 1-bit: 21 = 2 levels
  • 2-bit: 22 = 4 levels
  • 8-bit: 28 = 256 levels
  • 16-bit (CD Audio): 216 = 65,536 levels

Quantization Error

Because we are rounding, there's always a small difference between the actual sample value and the quantized level. This difference is called quantization error, which introduces noise or distortion into the digital signal. A higher bit depth means more levels, smaller steps, and less error.

3. Binary Encoding: The Language of Computers

What is Encoding?

The final step is to convert the numeric quantized values into binary code—a series of 1s and 0s. Each quantization level is assigned a unique binary number. The bit depth determines how many bits are used for each sample's code. For example, in a 3-bit system, level 0 might be `000`, level 1 `001`, and so on, up to level 7 (`111`). This stream of binary data is the final digital signal.

Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK)

What is ASK?

Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK) is a digital modulation technique where digital data is transmitted by varying the amplitude of a carrier signal. The frequency and phase remain constant, while different amplitudes represent different digital symbols (typically 0 and 1).

Mathematical Representation

For binary ASK (BASK):

s₁(t) = A₁ cos(2πf_c t) for bit '1'

s₀(t) = A₀ cos(2πf_c t) for bit '0'

Where A₁ > A₀ (often A₀ = 0)

Key Characteristics

  • Simplicity: Easy to implement and demodulate
  • Bandwidth: Same as the message signal bandwidth
  • Power Efficiency: Poor compared to PSK
  • Noise Sensitivity: Susceptible to amplitude variations

Frequency Shift Keying (FSK)

What is FSK?

Frequency Shift Keying (FSK) is a digital modulation scheme where digital information is transmitted by changing the frequency of a carrier signal. The amplitude and phase remain constant, while different frequencies represent different digital symbols.

Mathematical Representation

For binary FSK (BFSK):

s₁(t) = A cos(2πf₁ t) for bit '1'

s₀(t) = A cos(2πf₀ t) for bit '0'

Where f₁ ≠ f₀ are the two carrier frequencies

Advantages

  • Constant Amplitude: Less sensitive to amplitude variations
  • Non-coherent Detection: Can be detected without phase reference
  • Better Noise Performance: Than ASK in many scenarios
  • Spectral Efficiency: Good for narrowband applications

Phase Shift Keying (PSK)

What is PSK?

Phase Shift Keying (PSK) is a digital modulation technique where digital data is encoded by changing the phase of a reference signal. The amplitude and frequency remain constant, making it more robust against noise compared to ASK.

Mathematical Representation

For binary PSK (BPSK):

s₁(t) = A cos(2πf_c t + 0) for bit '1'

s₀(t) = A cos(2πf_c t + π) for bit '0'

Phase difference of 180° between symbols

Types of PSK

  • BPSK: Binary PSK (2 phases, 1 bit/symbol)
  • QPSK: Quadrature PSK (4 phases, 2 bits/symbol)
  • 8-PSK: 8 phases, 3 bits/symbol
  • DPSK: Differential PSK (phase relative to previous)

Advantages

  • Power Efficiency: Best among linear modulations
  • Spectral Efficiency: High data rate per bandwidth
  • Noise Immunity: Constant amplitude helps with noise
